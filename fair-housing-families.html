Can an Oklahoma Landlord Refuse to Rent to a Family With Kids?

What the October 2026 Oklahoma City settlement means for small landlords, and how to write listings, answer inquiries and set occupancy rules without shutting out families.

Informational only — not legal advice. Law summaries are plain English, not the statute text; read the section and talk to an attorney before relying on it. Items marked Practice are operating habits, not law.

Legal points last verified: October 27, 2026, against the federal Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C.), 24 CFR Part 100, 25 O.S. on Justia, and Justice Department sources. Laws change; read the section before relying on it.

1. Short answer

No, not because they have children. The federal Fair Housing Act bans discrimination based on familial status, which covers households with children under 18, pregnant people and people securing custody. Oklahoma law (25 O.S. §1452) bans it too. You can apply neutral, reasonable occupancy limits and screening criteria, but you can't turn a family away for having kids.

2. What happened in the Oklahoma City case

What DOJ alleges

  • The lawsuit alleges that in July 2022 a single mother asked about a one-bedroom at The Vintage at Mesta Park, a 36-unit complex in Oklahoma City, and was turned away once the property learned her then-three-year-old son would live with her.
  • DOJ alleges Jacqueline Fox, who managed the property and owned the company that owned it, told her no children had lived there in over eight years and the property was not "kid friendly."
  • Source: DOJ press release, Oct. 22, 2026 · DOJ case page

How it got to court

  • The woman filed a complaint with HUD. HUD investigated, issued a charge of discrimination and referred the case to the Justice Department.
  • DOJ sued in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Oklahoma in August 2025.

Settlement terms

  • On October 22, 2026, the owners agreed to pay $52,500 to the woman.
  • They also agreed to adopt a nondiscrimination policy and complete fair housing training.

3. Does this apply to a small landlord?

Federal law

  • "Familial status" means one or more children under 18 living with a parent, legal custodian or someone with their written permission. It also covers pregnant people and anyone securing legal custody of a child. 42 U.S.C. §3602(k) (Cornell LII)
  • Some small owners are exempt from parts of the Act: certain single-family homes rented by an owner of no more than three, without a broker or agent, and owner-occupied buildings with four or fewer units. 42 U.S.C. §3603(b) (Cornell LII)
  • Even exempt owners are covered by the advertising ban. A discriminatory listing is a problem regardless of size. 42 U.S.C. §3604(c) (Cornell LII)

Does Oklahoma have its own law?

  • Yes. Oklahoma separately bans familial-status discrimination in housing, including in advertising. 25 O.S. §1452 (Justia)
  • Oklahoma has its own exemptions in 25 O.S. §1453 (Justia). Don't assume they match the federal ones; read the section and ask counsel.
  • Complaints under state law go to the Oklahoma Attorney General's Office of Civil Rights Enforcement.
  • For what did and didn't change in this year's session, see Oklahoma landlord-tenant law in 2026. For the lease rules themselves, see the Oklahoma Landlord-Tenant Act overview.

4. Listing and inquiry language to avoid

Clear risks

  • "No kids," "adults only" and "not kid friendly" state a preference against families and are clear advertising risks. 42 U.S.C. §3604(c) (Cornell LII); 24 CFR 100.75
  • The same goes for what you say on the phone, by text or at a showing.

Softer phrases

  • Phrases like "perfect for a single professional" or "ideal for a couple" can signal a preference against families. Describe the unit, not the tenant.

Practice

  • Describe the property: bedrooms, square feet, parking, rent, deposit, pet policy.
  • Use one inquiry script for every caller, and ask everyone the same questions.
  • Read every listing once before it goes up, looking for words about who should live there.

5. Occupancy limits vs limiting kids

What HUD looks at

  • HUD's Keating memo (63 FR 70256, Dec. 18, 1998) treats two people per bedroom as a general starting point, not a rule.
  • Whether a limit is reasonable depends on factors including unit size, the ages of the children, the unit's configuration and local code.

Local code

  • Cities set their own occupancy rules through their housing or property maintenance codes. Check your city code before setting a limit; this page doesn't list local numbers.

Practice

  • Write one occupancy standard that counts people, not adults or children, and apply it to every unit and applicant.

6. Rent, deposits, rules and unit assignment

Risks

  • Charging families higher rent, a bigger deposit or extra fees because of children. 42 U.S.C. §3604(b) (Cornell LII); 24 CFR 100.65
  • Rules that apply only to children, such as a blanket ban on kids at the pool or playing outside. 24 CFR 100.65
  • Steering families to certain units, floors or buildings, or away from others. 24 CFR 100.70

What works

  • Reasonable safety rules that apply to everyone of every age, such as pool hours or no glass at the pool.
  • Deposits follow the same rules for every tenant: see Oklahoma security deposit rules.

7. Senior (55+/62+) housing exemption

Housing for Older Persons Act

  • Senior housing can exclude families with children only if it qualifies. 42 U.S.C. §3607(b) (Cornell LII); 24 CFR 100.300–100.308
  • 62+ housing: all occupants are 62 or older.
  • 55+ housing: at least 80% of occupied units have at least one occupant 55 or older, the property publishes and follows policies showing that intent, and it verifies ages.
  • Calling a building "adults only" doesn't make it senior housing.

8. Small-landlord checklist

Practice

  • Written screening criteria (income, history, references), applied the same way to every applicant
  • A standard inquiry script for calls, texts and showings
  • A listing review before every post
  • A written occupancy standard based on people per unit
  • A record of every application and the reason for each decision
  • Fair housing training for anyone who answers inquiries for you
  • See also Screening resources in the directory

9. If a complaint is filed

Deadlines

  • HUD complaints: within 1 year of the alleged discrimination. 42 U.S.C. §3610 (Cornell LII) · HUD: file a complaint
  • Private lawsuit: within 2 years. 42 U.S.C. §3613 (Cornell LII)
  • Oklahoma Attorney General: within 1 year. 25 O.S. §1502.2 (Justia) · AG Office of Civil Rights Enforcement
  • DOJ also takes tips at 1-800-896-7743.

Practice

  • Call an attorney before you respond.
  • Keep your listings, inquiry notes, applications and decision records.
  • Don't contact the person who filed about the complaint, and don't change their lease terms in response.

FAQ

Can I refuse a tenant because they have a child?

No. Refusing to rent because a household includes a child under 18 is familial-status discrimination under the federal Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. §3602(k), §3604) and Oklahoma law (25 O.S. §1452). Apply the same written screening criteria to every applicant.

Can I limit how many children live in a unit?

You can set a reasonable occupancy limit on the total number of people, applied to everyone. A limit aimed at children is a risk. HUD's Keating memo treats two people per bedroom as a general starting point, not a rule; unit size, children's ages, configuration and local code all matter. Check your city code.

Can I list my rental as adults-only?

Not unless the property qualifies as senior housing under the Housing for Older Persons Act (42 U.S.C. §3607(b)). Phrases like "adults only," "no kids" or "not kid friendly" are clear advertising risks under 42 U.S.C. §3604(c).

Do fair housing rules apply if I only own one or two rentals?

Some small owners are exempt from parts of the federal Act, such as certain owner-occupied buildings of up to four units and some single-family homes rented without a broker (42 U.S.C. §3603(b)). The advertising ban still applies to them, and Oklahoma has its own law with its own exemptions (25 O.S. §1452–1453). Ask an attorney before relying on an exemption.

Can I charge more for families with kids?

Higher rent, a bigger deposit or extra fees because a household has children are a risk under 42 U.S.C. §3604(b) and 24 CFR 100.65. Charge the same terms to every household.

What did the Oklahoma City landlord agree to?

According to the Justice Department, the owners of The Vintage at Mesta Park agreed on October 22, 2026, to pay $52,500 to the woman, adopt a nondiscrimination policy and complete fair housing training.
