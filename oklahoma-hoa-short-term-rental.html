Can an HOA Ban Your Airbnb in Oklahoma?

You found a lake house and want to know whether an HOA can stop a short-term rental listing. On November 10, 2026, the Oklahoma Supreme Court held 5–4 in Silverhawk Homeowners Association, Inc. v. Tawose, 2026 OK 82, that one homeowner's short-term rental violated that neighborhood's recorded covenant requiring private-residence use and prohibiting a "store or business." The Court affirmed an injunction and a $8,528 judgment for HOA "Specific Assessments" (fines and penalties under the declaration, not attorney fees). This is not a statewide ban, and it does not decide what every Oklahoma HOA covenant means. The result is tied to this record and this covenant.

What this does not mean

  • Not a statewide short-term-rental ban. The Court did not enact a statute and did not say every Oklahoma Airbnb is illegal.
  • Not a rule that every Oklahoma HOA may prohibit short-term rentals. The issue was whether "a particular restrictive real estate covenant" prohibited this use (2026 OK 82, ¶1). Another neighborhood's wording may be narrower, broader, silent, or different in ways that matter.
  • Not a substitute for reading your own documents. Silverhawk's Amended Declaration is not your declaration. A welcome packet, a Facebook group rule, or a verbal "the board said it's fine" is not the recorded covenant.
  • Not a decision that "Airbnb" or "short-term rental" must appear in a covenant for a board to try to enforce it — and not a decision that every "business" or "private residence" clause will come out the same way. This majority found this language clear and unambiguous as applied here (¶0, ¶46). Justice Jett wrote a dissent joined by three other justices (¶104).
  • Not a ruling on Fort Gibson Lake, Wagoner, or other local rules. Ordinances, lodging tax, and permits are separate checks. Silverhawk is a neighborhood inside Oklahoma City limits (homes there have Edmond mailing addresses) and was addressed under Oklahoma City zoning (¶7, ¶43–¶44).
  • Not legal advice, and not a prediction of what a court would do with a different covenant, a different set of facts, or a later revision of this opinion.

What the Court actually decided

The case is Silverhawk Homeowners Association, Inc. v. Adewale Eniola Tawose, 2026 OK 82, Case No. 122129, decided November 10, 2026. Silverhawk is a neighborhood inside Oklahoma City limits (homes there have Edmond mailing addresses). The HOA filed suit on Nov. 5, 2021 (Oklahoma County CJ-2021-4778), and Tawose was served on Nov. 16, 2021. After a five-day bench trial, Oklahoma County District Judge Sheila Stinson entered an injunction and declaratory judgment for the association (¶0, ¶5). The Court of Civil Appeals, Division II, reversed in part, vacated in part, and remanded in a 2–1 opinion on July 14, 2025 (OSCN docket; ¶13). The Supreme Court vacated that opinion and affirmed the district court (¶0, ¶14, ¶102).

The question was whether a particular restrictive covenant prohibited this short-term rental because of the commercial nature of the use (¶1). The Court held that the homeowner's commercial activities violated "clear and unambiguous language in a restrictive covenant," that challenges to the fines and assessment lacked merit, that the injunction was not against the clear weight of the evidence, that attorney-fee challenges were not preserved, and that denying a new trial was not an abuse of discretion (¶0, ¶102). It reviewed the trial court's findings for whether they were against the clear weight of the evidence (¶46), so the result is tied to this record and this covenant.

About this operation — not a test for every listing — the Court recited that the homeowner admitted operating a short-term rental; that from late 2021 to late 2023 the evidence showed 36 stays and 141 people; and that the property was advertised online for money, with check-in and check-out times, sundries, a cleaner after each stay, and hotel or motel taxes (¶5, ¶8–¶9, ¶32–¶33). The district court awarded $8,528 in HOA "Specific Assessments" (fines and penalties allowed by the declaration, not attorney fees) (¶10 & n.1), enjoined the short-term rental business, and found a nuisance tied to late-night gatherings and disruptive behavior (¶10). The Supreme Court affirmed the injunction and the assessment award (¶2, ¶102). The attorney-fee award was not reviewed (¶101–¶102).

Justice Edmondson wrote the majority, joined by Chief Justice Rowe and Justices Winchester, Combs, and Darby (¶103). Justice Jett wrote a dissent joined by three other justices (Vice Chief Justice Kuehn and Justices Gurich and Kane) (¶104).

The covenant language that mattered

The text at issue is from Silverhawk's Amended Declaration, Article II, Section 1 (Use), filed January 26, 2017, with the Oklahoma County Clerk (¶4):

Section 1. Use. The Lots in Silverhawk shall be used for private residence purposes only. No store or business, no gas or automobile service station, no flat, duplex, or apartment house, though intended for residential purposes, and no building of any kind whatsoever shall be erected or maintained thereon, except private dwelling houses, and such dwelling house in its entirety being designated for occupancy by a single family.

The majority read "private residence purposes only," "no store or business," and single-family occupancy "in its entirety" together, and treated "business" as having a plain and ordinary meaning (¶37, ¶40, ¶46). It rejected the argument that "business" is facially ambiguous. In the Court's words: "Tawose's argument is an attack upon originalism as a method for construing legal documents" (¶34). The opinion contrasts serial short-term occupancy with long-term single-family leases used as someone's residence, a line the HOA drew and the Court discussed (¶38–¶40). The HOA asked the homeowner to convert to a long-term rental, and two other short-term operators did so (¶45) — a fact of this dispute, not a rule that long-term always complies. The hotel-like facts in this record help explain why this operation was treated as commercial; they are not a universal test (¶8–¶9, ¶32–¶33).

What lake hosts and buyers should read before they buy or list

This checklist is diligence, not legal advice, for hosts and buyers around Fort Gibson Lake (near Wagoner, OK) and elsewhere in Oklahoma. The holding is about Silverhawk's declaration — not lake or Wagoner ordinances.

  • Is the house in an HOA, addition, PUD, or other association? Many lake houses are; do not assume yes or no.
  • Get the recorded declaration, plus amendments, bylaws, and rules — not just listing remarks or a seller summary.
  • Read for: private or residential use only; "business," "commercial," "store," or "trade"; short-term rental, transient, hotel, boarding, or home-sharing words; single-family or "in its entirety" occupancy; any minimum lease length; nuisance, parking, signs, trash, and quiet hours; and enforcement and fine provisions.
  • See how the documents treat long-term leasing versus short-term or transient stays. Converting to a long-term rental was a fact of this dispute, not a guarantee that long-term always passes (¶45).
  • Check the amendment procedure: who can change the covenants, by what vote or process, and whether later amendments or board rules apply to your lot.
  • Practice: keep a file of your rental history and HOA communications (listings, stay records, tax filings, emails, written board positions). The court recited advertising, stay counts, and taxes in this record (¶8–¶9, ¶32).
  • Do not assume "it doesn't say Airbnb, so it's allowed," or that every "no business" clause comes out like Silverhawk. This majority found this language clear and unambiguous as applied here (¶46). Justice Jett wrote a dissent joined by three other justices (¶104). Different wording can lead to a different result.
  • Practice: ask your insurance agent whether your policy covers short-term rental use. Coverage is a contract question, not something this opinion decided.
  • If you are buying to list, make review of the recorded covenants (and any HOA estoppel or resale package) a contract condition, and have an Oklahoma attorney read them before you are bound. If you already host, do the same read before a new listing or rule change, and do not rely on an old informal okay.

City, tax, and platform rules are a separate stack

HOA covenants are not the same as city or county zoning, a home-sharing permit, or lodging or sales tax. The opinion discusses Oklahoma City home-sharing and transient-rental rules and a 2023 application to the Oklahoma City Board of Adjustment (¶7, ¶43–¶44). A Fort Gibson Lake address near Wagoner will be under different local rules. Check those rules and tax registration separately, and talk to counsel or a tax professional. Platform rules are another layer.

This dispute is about recorded covenants, not the Oklahoma Residential Landlord and Tenant Act. For statute-cited landlord-tenant questions, see RentStatute's Oklahoma pages. Those statutes do not decide an HOA covenant question like this one.

FAQ

Can an HOA ban my Airbnb in Oklahoma?

It depends on the recorded covenants and other applicable rules, not on a statewide ban. In 2026 OK 82, the Court affirmed that Silverhawk's particular covenant was violated by that operation. Your documents may read differently. Talk to an Oklahoma attorney.

Did Oklahoma ban short-term rentals?

No. This decision is not a statewide statutory ban. The Court decided whether one neighborhood's particular covenant prohibited this use (¶1).

What language should I look for in my covenants?

Residential-use limits, "business" or commercial restrictions, any short-term or minimum-stay terms, single-family or occupancy language, enforcement and fine provisions, and amendments. The quote above is Silverhawk's text, not a form to assume is in your declaration.

If my covenants don't mention Airbnb or "short-term rental," am I automatically allowed to list?

Not automatically. This case was decided on "private residence" and "business" wording, which the majority called clear and unambiguous as applied here (¶0, ¶46). Different wording can lead to a different result. Have an attorney read your documents.

Does this ruling apply to a Fort Gibson Lake house near Wagoner?

The holding is about the Silverhawk declaration — Silverhawk is a neighborhood inside Oklahoma City limits (homes there have Edmond mailing addresses) — not lake or Wagoner ordinances. If your lake house is in an association, read your recorded covenants. Local tax and zoning are a separate check.

What did the dissenting justices say?

Justice Travis Jett dissented, joined by Vice Chief Justice Kuehn and Justices Gurich and Kane. He wrote that the decision "places Oklahoma in the minority of jurisdictions that have considered how restrictive covenants apply to short-term rentals," and that owners "may contractually prohibit short-term rentals by restrictive covenant, but this must be accomplished by plain prohibitory language in an agreed upon covenant" (2026 OK 82, Jett, J., dissenting, ¶1).
